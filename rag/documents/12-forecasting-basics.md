# How weather forecasts are made

> Source: WMO guidance on numerical weather prediction, public documentation

## Numerical weather prediction

Forecasts start with observations from surface stations, weather balloons, aircraft, ships, buoys, radar and satellites. These are combined into a best estimate of the current atmosphere in a step called data assimilation. Physics equations describing motion, heat and moisture are then solved forward in time on a three-dimensional grid.

## Why forecasts lose skill with time

Small errors in the starting state grow, which limits useful detailed prediction to roughly a week to ten days. Forecast centres run ensembles, many slightly different simulations, to measure how confident a forecast is. When ensemble members agree, confidence is high; when they diverge, the forecast is uncertain and probabilities become more informative than single values.

## Different models, different answers

Major global models include ECMWF IFS from Europe, GFS from NOAA in the United States, and ICON from DWD in Germany. They differ in resolution, physics and assimilation, so they often disagree on the timing and placement of rainfall. Comparing models is a legitimate way to gauge uncertainty. Disagreement usually means lower confidence rather than one model being wrong.

## Reading a forecast well

Treat timing to within a few hours rather than to the minute, expect placement errors of tens of kilometres for convective rain, and pay attention to the probability and to the range between minimum and maximum values rather than to a single headline number.
