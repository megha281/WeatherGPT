# Humidity, dew point and comfort

> Source: WMO Guide to Meteorological Instruments and Methods of Observation, general meteorological reference

## Relative humidity

Relative humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature. Because warm air holds more vapour, relative humidity falls during the day as temperature rises, even when the actual moisture content does not change. This is why morning readings are usually higher than afternoon readings.

## Dew point

Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated and condensation begins. Unlike relative humidity, it measures the absolute moisture in the air and is a better guide to how muggy conditions feel:

- Below 13°C: dry and comfortable
- 13 to 16°C: comfortable
- 16 to 20°C: becoming noticeable
- 20 to 24°C: humid and uncomfortable
- Above 24°C: oppressive, with reduced ability to cool by sweating

## Why it matters for safety

High humidity reduces evaporative cooling, raising heat stress at a given temperature. It also supports fog formation when temperatures drop overnight to near the dew point, and it is one of the ingredients thunderstorms need.

## Fog and visibility

When air cools to its dew point near the surface, fog forms. Radiation fog is common on clear calm nights, especially in winter over north India, and usually clears a few hours after sunrise. Visibility below 1000 metres is classified as fog; below 200 metres it seriously affects road, rail and air travel.
